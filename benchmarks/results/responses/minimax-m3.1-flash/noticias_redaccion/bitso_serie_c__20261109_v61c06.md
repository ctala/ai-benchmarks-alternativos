# Bitso y la nueva infraestructura para mover dinero entre países

Bitso demonstrates that the international payments market still has room for new models. The Mexican cryptocurrency platform raised $250 million in a Series C round led by Tiger Global and Coatue, reaching a valuation of $2.2 billion. With this operation, it became Mexico’s first crypto unicorn.

The company’s trajectory is especially relevant for startups that process international payments. Its story combines several priorities: access to a large regional market, products that simplify the use of digital assets, early investment in regulatory compliance and a clear value proposition for users who may not know how the underlying technology works.

## A $250 million round and Mexico’s first crypto unicorn

Bitso is a Mexico-based cryptocurrency platform founded by Daniel Vogel, Pablo González and Ben Peters. Through its platform, users can buy, sell and store cryptocurrencies such as bitcoin and ether using Mexican pesos and other local currencies.

The Series C round was led by Tiger Global and Coatue. Both firms were among the most active investors in Latin American startup rounds during that period, with investments spanning fintech, e-commerce and software.

The size of the round and the resulting valuation reflect the strategic importance that investors assigned to the company. For the Mexican startup ecosystem, Bitso also marked a milestone by becoming the country’s first crypto unicorn.

Its growth, however, has been connected to more than the speculative demand for digital assets. Remittances and the need to move money across borders have been central use cases for the platform.

## Remittances: a large corridor with room for better alternatives

One of the uses that Bitso has most actively promoted is sending remittances. People living in the United States can send money to relatives in Mexico through cryptocurrencies, which are later converted into pesos.

According to the company, this mechanism can be faster and cheaper than some traditional money transfer services. That proposition addresses two recurring friction points for users: the time required to complete a transfer and the cost of sending money through conventional channels.

The remittance corridor between the United States and Mexico is one of the largest in the world. Many Mexican workers send money to their families every month. Traditionally, these transfers have been made through specialized companies or banks that charge commissions and apply unfavorable exchange rates.

Bitso’s founders identified an opportunity when buying cryptocurrencies in Latin America was still a complicated and unreliable process. Instead of approaching the market only as a place to trade volatile assets, the company built a platform that allows people to use local currencies and focuses on practical financial needs.

This distinction is important. The use case is not limited to users who already understand how digital assets work. Remittance senders need a service that makes it possible to move money home, while recipients in Mexico can ultimately receive pesos.

## Compliance as part of the product

From the beginning, Bitso presented itself as a platform that works with authorities and applies controls to prevent money laundering. For the company, this approach was key to gaining the trust of users and institutions.

That decision also illustrates a broader lesson for payment startups. Building on new infrastructure requires more than a technically functional product. Companies must invest early in compliance and maintain a relationship with regulators.

For international payments, trust is part of the value proposition. Users need to feel that the service is responsible, regulated and capable of handling cross-border transactions. The same applies to companies that want to receive or send payments through cryptocurrency-based products.

Bitso’s approach also demonstrates that compliance can be integrated into the platform’s positioning rather than treated solely as an internal requirement. Working with authorities and applying anti-money-laundering controls formed part of the company’s strategy for building credibility in a market where cryptocurrency adoption was still developing.

## Services for companies and payments users may not need to understand

In addition to its application for individuals, Bitso offers services for companies. These tools allow other businesses to send and receive payments using cryptocurrencies as a transfer method.

In this model, the end user may not even know that the money passed through a digital asset. The visible result is simply that the payment arrived faster. This is a significant element of the company’s offer because it removes much of the technological complexity from the customer experience.

For businesses, the attraction is not necessarily to operate with cryptocurrency directly. The infrastructure can serve as a means to improve how a payment is processed while presenting a simple experience to the person who sends or receives it.

This is a useful reference for startups in international payments. The adoption of new infrastructure does not always require users to become familiar with the underlying technology. It is enough for the product to solve a clear problem and make the underlying process less complicated.

## Stablecoins as an alternative to volatility

Bitso has also promoted the use of stablecoins, cryptocurrencies whose value is tied to the dollar. According to the company, these assets allow users to protect themselves from the volatility of other digital assets and from the depreciation of local currencies.

This characteristic is particularly relevant in cross-border payments. Bitcoin and ether can experience significant price changes over short periods, while the dollar-linked value of stablecoins offers a different approach for users who need to transfer or preserve money.

For a company that processes international payments, this creates an additional design possibility: combine the convenience of digital assets with a value that users can relate to a widely used reference currency. The source of the value is the dollar, while the method of transfer is based on cryptocurrency infrastructure.

Bitso’s support for both volatile assets and stablecoins reflects two sides of its business. The platform gives users access to cryptocurrencies such as bitcoin and ether, but it also develops products aimed at practical needs such as remittances, where users generally care more about the transfer itself than about trading digital assets.

## Regional expansion and use of the capital

Beyond Mexico, Bitso operates in other Latin American countries, including Argentina and Brazil. The company said it would use the capital raised in the Series C round to expand across the region, develop new products and strengthen its regulatory compliance areas.

This focus reflects the scale of the opportunity. International money movement is not limited to a single corridor, and each market has its own local currencies, payment habits and regulatory framework. Bitso’s presence in Mexico, Argentina and Brazil positions the company to address a regional problem rather than serving only one bilateral route.

The participation of Tiger Global and Coatue also places the round within the broader growth of Latin American startups. Both investors had been active in fintech, e-commerce and software, sectors closely connected to the development of digital infrastructure in the region.

## Volatility and regulation remain central challenges

The cryptocurrency sector is volatile. Digital asset prices can change sharply within short periods, and those movements affect transaction volumes and platform revenues. For any payment company operating in this ecosystem, market volatility is not merely an external phenomenon: it has a direct impact on business performance.

Regulation also varies from one country to another and continues to develop across much of the region. A company that wants to expand its payment products must therefore navigate different regulatory environments while maintaining internal controls and relationships with local authorities.

These challenges make early investment in compliance even more relevant. As Bitso’s case shows, a new payment infrastructure cannot be evaluated only by its technological novelty or the size of its funding round. Sustainability also depends on regulatory readiness, user confidence and the ability to manage the risks associated with digital assets.

## What founders can learn from Bitso

For founders working on international payments, Bitso shows that the problem of moving money between countries remains open and that there is room for new models. The company identified a large, recurring need—monthly remittances between the United States and Mexico—and addressed it with a product based on cryptocurrency infrastructure.

Its trajectory highlights three priorities. First, start with a clear user problem: transferring money quickly and at a potentially lower cost. Second, make the technology invisible when possible, so the customer perceives a faster and simpler payment rather than the complexity of cryptocurrency. Third, treat compliance as an essential part of the product and build relationships with authorities from the beginning.

Bitso’s $250 million round, its $2.2 billion valuation and its status as Mexico’s first crypto unicorn demonstrate the potential of that approach. The opportunity for payment startups is not only to introduce a new technology, but to use it to solve existing friction in the movement of money across borders.
